The Discipline Test

Your draft strategy has an integration idea but you're still not sure how AI will actually perform in your disciplinary context. This pathway uses your expert knowledge to evaluate AI output critically — and determine whether the tool you're imagining can do what you need it to do.

1

Identify the specific task from your draft strategy

Look at the integration idea you described this morning. What is the actual student-facing task — the question they'd ask AI, the thing they'd use it to produce or evaluate? Start there, not with a generic version of it.

2

Run it through an AI tool and evaluate as an expert

Use the prompt below — it includes a placeholder for your draft strategy context. Then read the response the way your students would encounter it. What does it get right? What does it miss, flatten, or oversimplify in ways your lens insights predicted?

3

Document whether your integration idea holds up

Does this AI output support the integration you described in your draft? Does it expose the concern you named? Note what you'd need to change about your plan based on what you saw.

Discipline Test Prompt
I'm a university instructor testing whether an AI tool can support a specific integration idea I'm developing. Here is my draft integration plan: [Paste or summarize your Draft Strategy submission here — your course context, integration idea, what you want students to gain, and the concern you named] The specific task I want to test: [describe the exact thing a student would ask AI to do in this integration — e.g., "generate a first draft of a lab report discussion section based on this data"] Please respond the way you would if a student in my course submitted this request directly. --- After you respond, I'll evaluate your output as a disciplinary expert. I'll be looking at: accuracy for my field, whether it would actually support the learning outcome I described, and whether it exposes or sidesteps the concern I named in my draft strategy. Then I'll tell you what I found and we'll discuss what this means for my integration plan.
After the AI responds: Ask — "Based on my integration plan and this output, what specific change to my assignment design would best address the concern I named?"
Your artifact for share-out

A brief written comparison: what the AI got right or wrong against your disciplinary standard, and one specific revision to your draft integration plan based on what you found.

Run It as a Student

Your draft strategy describes an integration idea you're ready to stress-test. This pathway asks you to sit in the student seat — use AI the way your students would, on the actual task you designed, and see whether the learning outcome you described holds up.

1

Pull the student-facing task from your draft strategy

Your draft describes an assignment, activity, or assessment. What does it look like from the student's side? Use the actual prompt or task description — not a summary of it.

2

Complete it using AI, the way a student would

Use the prompt below — it includes a placeholder for your draft plan. Don't use your expert knowledge to guide it. Approximate how a typical student in your course would actually use AI for this task.

3

Evaluate it against the learning outcome you named this morning

Does this output demonstrate what you wanted students to learn? Does it expose the concern you flagged in your draft? What would you need to change about your design?

Run It as a Student Prompt
I'm going to give you a student assignment from one of my courses. I want you to complete it the way a student would — using AI assistance as I intend to allow it in my integration plan. Here is my draft integration plan: [Paste or summarize your Draft Strategy submission — your course context, integration idea, learning outcome, AI's intended role, and the concern you named] The student-facing task: [paste or describe the specific assignment prompt, question, or task the student would receive] The AI's intended role as I've designed it: [e.g., "students may use AI to generate a first draft, but must revise it using their own analysis" — be as specific as your draft allows] Please complete the task now, within those constraints. --- After you respond, I'll evaluate the output as the instructor: Does it meet the learning outcome I described? Does it expose or sidestep the concern I named? What would I need to change about the assignment design?
Follow-up to try: "Based on my integration plan and this student output, what one change to my assignment design would best protect the learning outcome I described while still using AI in the way I intended?"
Your artifact for share-out

The AI's student output alongside your evaluation: does it meet your learning outcome? Does it confirm or change the concern you named? Include at least one specific revision to your draft integration plan.

Build

Build One Prompt, Test It Twice

Your draft strategy identifies a specific moment where students need support and you're ready to build something reusable. This pathway asks you to design a prompt for that moment, test it twice, and iterate — producing an artifact another instructor could pick up and use.

1

Name the moment from your draft strategy

Your draft describes a point in your course where AI could support students. Get more specific: not "during the writing process" but "when students move from summarizing a source to constructing their own argument." The more precisely you name the moment, the more useful the prompt will be.

2

Build and test your prompt once

Use the framework below — it includes a placeholder for your draft strategy. Run the prompt and evaluate: Does it scaffold without doing the thinking? Does it address the concern you named? Is it specific enough to your context?

3

Revise based on what your lens insights predicted — then test again

Change one thing: a constraint, the framing, the level of scaffolding, or the question the AI leads with. Run it a second time. Document what changed between runs and connect it back to the lens insight that motivated the revision.

Prompt Builder Framework
I'm building a reusable AI prompt for a specific moment in my course where students need support. Here is the context from my draft integration plan: [Paste or summarize your Draft Strategy — your course context, the moment of integration, what you want students to gain, the lens insights that shaped your thinking, and the concern you named] THE SPECIFIC MOMENT: [narrow it down further — e.g., "when students need to move from summarizing a secondary source to constructing their own historical argument"] WHAT I WANT THE AI TO DO: [e.g., "ask the student three questions that help them identify the gap between what the source says and what they want to argue, without generating the argument for them"] WHAT I DON'T WANT THE AI TO DO: [e.g., "do not write the thesis or provide a model argument the student could copy — this is where my concern about overreliance lives"] THE STUDENT WILL START BY SAYING: [e.g., "I've read my sources and I have notes but I don't know how to start my argument."] Please act as the AI tool students would use in this moment. Begin the interaction now. --- After this first run, I'll evaluate the output, revise the prompt with one change informed by my lens insights, and run a second test to compare.
Between your two tests: Connect your revision to one of the lenses from this morning. If you change the constraint because of an overreliance concern — that's a critical AI literacy or student engagement lens revision. Name it. That reasoning is part of your artifact.
Your artifact for share-out

Your two prompt versions, the two AI outputs side by side, the one change you made between runs, and which lens insight motivated that change. Written so another instructor in a different discipline could adapt and use it.

🎤 Share-out — 2:40 PM

Each group shares (3–4 minutes):

  • Which pathway you worked in and what you focused on
  • The artifact you produced — show it, don't just describe it
  • One thing the student panel made you think about differently — and whether it showed up in your AI session
  • One thing you're taking back to your course design
The goal of the share-out is not polish — it's specificity. The cohort learns more from a real finding ("the AI flattened my discipline's standard of evidence in a way students wouldn't catch") than from a general impression ("it was pretty good but had some issues").
