Session goal

Build your presentation from your draft strategy

You've just heard from the measurement panel. Use this session to develop your project presentation and Canvas document directly from your draft integration plan — no AI tools required.

⏱ 70 min total
📋 Presentation + Canvas doc
🎤 5–7 min share-out
Required elements — include all six
1

Your course and the problem you are solving

Name the course, level, and the specific instructional problem or gap that motivated this integration idea. Be concrete — your colleagues should be able to picture the course and the students.

e.g., "HIST 300, upper-division historiography. Students consistently struggle to move from summarizing sources to constructing their own argument."

2

Your AI integration plan

Describe where AI enters the student experience, what the student's role is, and what AI's role is. Be specific about the task — not "students will use AI to improve their writing" but what they will actually do and when.

e.g., "Students submit a rough argument to an AI tool, which asks three clarifying questions. Students revise based on their own responses — not the AI's suggestions."

3

What you want students to learn

State the specific skill, capacity, or understanding this integration is designed to develop. One clear sentence is enough — avoid phrases like "use AI effectively" in favor of the actual cognitive or disciplinary work you want students to do.

e.g., "Students will develop the ability to distinguish between a source's claim and their own interpretive argument."

4

How you will know if it's working

Describe your measurement approach — what you will collect, when, and how you will analyze it. This does not need to be a formal research design. It should be practical and tied to your course.

e.g., "I'll compare the thesis quality in student papers before and after the integration using a rubric focused on evidence-to-claim reasoning. I'll also do a mid-semester check-in survey."

5

The challenge you haven't solved

Name the design tension, risk, or open question that is still unresolved. Be honest — this is not a weakness to hide, it's the thing you want your colleagues' input on. Generic concerns ("AI might be misused") are less useful than specific ones.

e.g., "Students who've used AI extensively will navigate the tool very differently than students who haven't. I don't yet know how to account for that gap without removing the scaffold."

6

One question for the cohort

End your presentation with the one thing you most need input on. It should be specific enough that a colleague can respond with something useful — not just encouragement.

e.g., "Has anyone found a way to build in accountability for the revision step without making it feel punitive or formulaic?"

Format — keep it simple

Your presentation does not need to be a polished slide deck. Use whatever format helps you present clearly in 5–7 minutes:

  • A Google Doc with the six elements as labeled sections
  • A simple slide deck (one slide per element)
  • A shared document you walk the cohort through
  • Notes you speak from directly
What matters most: specificity. Your colleagues learn more from a concrete finding tied to your course than from a polished presentation of a general idea. If you're short on time, prioritize elements 2, 4, and 5 — the integration plan, the measurement approach, and the honest challenge.
Canvas submission

Submit both of the following using the Start Assignment button in Canvas.

Text entry: Write your project document covering all six elements above. Aim for 300–400 words. Write in your own voice — specific to your course, honest about what's unresolved.

Link: Paste a link to your presentation outline, Google Doc, or slide deck — whatever you'll use during the share-out. It doesn't need to be polished, just followable.
