Level 1

Explore

"I'm still investigating what I think about these new tools."
⏱ 30 min in-room
👥 Self-selected group
🎯 Record reflections in Canvas
🔬The activity: The Discipline Test

Faculty use their own expertise as the evaluator. Each person picks something they know deeply from their field and asks two different AI tools the same question about it — then evaluates the responses not for general quality, but through an expert lens. The goal is curiosity, not judgment.

0'
0 – 5 min
Frame the activity
Introduce the premise: "You already know your field better than any AI does. Today you're the expert evaluator, not the student." Ask everyone to think of one concept, question, or claim from their discipline they'd feel confident evaluating. Open two AI tool tabs.
5'
5 – 20 min
Run the test
Each person asks the same question to two different tools. They take notes: Where is the AI confident but wrong? Where does it surprise them positively? Where does it feel authoritative but hollow? Encourage people to push back — ask the AI to justify its answer, or give a counterexample.
Example opening prompt
Explain [a concept from your discipline] as you would to an advanced undergraduate student. Be specific and avoid vague generalities.
Follow-up push prompt
What are the most common misconceptions about this topic that even advanced students hold? Give me specific examples, not just categories.
20'
20 – 30 min
Group debrief
Pairs or full group share what they found. Fellows anchor the conversation around the two debrief questions below — resist the urge to evaluate AI in general. Keep it specific to what each person found.
Debrief question 1
"What did you catch that a student in your course probably wouldn't? What would they have needed to know to catch it?"
Debrief question 2
"Was there anything the AI did better than you expected? What does that tell you?"
🚀Suggested tools

Have these open and ready at the start. Suggest pairs rather than all four at once.

Most promising use case discovered

One question or concern

✅Fellow prep checklist
  • Prepare one example from your own discipline — a question you'd feel comfortable evaluating — to model the activity at the start
  • Have two browser tabs open with different AI tools before the session begins
  • Keep the debrief question visible — on a slide or written on a whiteboard
  • Remind the group: no output required, no "right" conclusion. The goal is noticing.
  • If the group finishes early, prompt: "Ask the AI what it can't do well in your field" — this reliably extends the conversation
🚪The door this opens
💡
Faculty leave with a specific example of AI's disciplinary limitations — something they found themselves, not something they were told. That experience becomes the foundation for every conversation in the 8 Lenses activity that follows. Encourage them to try this again with a different topic or tool on their own time.
Level 2

Implement

"I have an integration idea I'd like to explore."
⏱ 30 min in-room
👥 Self-selected group
🎯 One design insight to take home
🎭The activity: Run It as a Student

Faculty complete their own assignment using AI — not as an instructor evaluating AI, but as a motivated student trying to produce a good result with AI support. The experience of sitting on the other side of the assignment reveals design gaps that are nearly impossible to see from the instructor's perspective.

0'
0 – 5 min
Set up and frame
Ask everyone to pull up an assignment they're thinking about integrating AI into — or describe one out loud if they don't have it handy. Frame the mindset shift: "For the next 15 minutes you are a student who wants to do well and is comfortable using AI. Your job is not to resist it — use it fully." Fellows should have a fallback assignment ready for anyone who doesn't bring one.
5'
5 – 20 min
Complete the assignment as a student
Faculty work through their assignment using AI — genuinely, not sarcastically. Encourage them to use follow-up prompts, ask for revisions, and try to produce something they'd consider submitting. The goal is to discover where AI makes the work easy and where they still had to think.
Student-mode starter prompt
I have this assignment: [paste your assignment]. Help me work through it step by step. Start by helping me understand what the instructor is really looking for, then help me build a strong response.
Push further
Make this response stronger. Where is it weakest? Revise those sections so the overall submission would receive the highest possible grade.
20'
20 – 30 min
Debrief as instructors
Switch back to instructor mode. The two debrief questions below anchor a fast, honest conversation. Fellows should resist turning this into a general AI ethics discussion — keep it focused on the specific assignment each person brought.
Debrief question 1
"If a student submitted what you just produced, what grade would it get — and how do you feel about that?"
Debrief question 2
"Where in the assignment did you still have to think? Is that the part you most want students to think about too?"
🚀Suggested tools

For this activity, one tool is enough — depth over breadth. ChatGPT and Claude both handle document uploads well if faculty want to paste a full assignment.

📝Canvas reflection prompt

Ask faculty to post a brief response to these three prompts in the Canvas discussion before moving to the 8 Lenses activity.

✅Fellow prep checklist
  • Prepare a fallback assignment — something from your own course — for anyone who arrives without one
  • Do the activity yourself beforehand with your own assignment. Your experience is the most useful facilitation material you'll have.
  • Frame the mindset shift clearly at the start — "student mode" is the whole mechanism of the activity
  • Watch for people who resist using AI fully. Gently push: "What would a student who really wanted a good grade do here?"
  • Keep the debrief tight — 10 minutes is enough if the questions are specific
🚪The door this opens
💡
Most faculty leave wanting to redesign something specific — a prompt, a constraint, a deliverable — in their actual assignment. That impulse is exactly where the 8 Lenses tool picks up. Encourage them to bring the assignment they tested into that activity and use the Engagement and Authentic Assessment lenses on it.
Level 3

Build

"I want to create something reusable for my students."
⏱ 30 min in-room
👥 Self-selected group
🎯 One tested, usable prompt
🔨The activity: Build One Prompt, Test It Twice

Faculty identify one specific moment in their course where students consistently struggle — a concept, a step in a project, a type of analysis — and build a prompt that scaffolds AI support for students at exactly that moment. The goal is one tested, usable prompt. Not a system. Not a policy. One good prompt a student could pick up and use without additional instructions.

Constraint: a student should be able to use this prompt without any additional instructions from you.
0'
0 – 5 min
Identify the moment
Each person names one specific place in their course where students get stuck — not a general topic, but a precise moment. "When they try to move from data to argument." "When they're writing their literature review introduction." "When they hit their first integration problem." Fellows should share their own example to model the specificity needed.
5'
5 – 15 min
Write and test the first version
Faculty write a draft prompt for a student to use at that moment and test it live. The test question: does the AI response actually help a student move forward, or does it do the work for them? Revise once based on what comes back.
Prompt template to start from
I am a student working on [describe the assignment or task]. I'm stuck at the point where [describe the specific moment of difficulty]. I don't want you to do this for me — instead, ask me three questions that will help me figure out the next step on my own.
15'
15 – 25 min
Stress-test and refine
Swap prompts with someone else in the group. Each person tries the other's prompt and reports back: "As a student, would this help me or let me off the hook?" Revise based on what comes back. Fellows circulate and watch for prompts that are either too open (AI does everything) or too closed (AI can't do anything useful).
Stress-test prompt to run on your own prompt
Here is a prompt I have written for students to use when they get stuck on an assignment: [paste your prompt]. Respond as a student who wants to do as little original thinking as possible. Show me how you would use this prompt to get a good result without really engaging with the problem yourself.
25'
25 – 30 min
Share and close
Each person reads their final prompt aloud in one sentence: what it's for and what it's designed to do. No lengthy explanation — if it needs explaining, it needs more revision. Fellows close with the door question below.
Closing question
"Where else in your course is there a moment like this — where one well-designed prompt could scaffold student thinking without replacing it?"
🚀Suggested tools

Claude and ChatGPT both handle iterative prompt refinement well. Encourage faculty to use the same tool throughout so they can see how revisions change the output.

✅Fellow prep checklist
  • Write and test your own example prompt before the session — bring it as a model, not as the answer
  • Keep the scope constraint visible throughout: "One prompt. One moment. A student could use it right now without asking you."
  • Watch for over-scoping — faculty who want to build a whole system. Redirect: "Start with the hardest moment. One prompt. You can build more later."
  • The peer swap in the middle is the most valuable part — protect that time even if writing runs long
  • Have a shared doc or slide ready so everyone can paste their final prompt — it becomes a small collective prompt library to take home
🚪The door this opens
💡
Faculty who complete this activity realize that a prompt is a design artifact — it has intention, constraints, and consequences, just like an assignment does. That reframe is the bridge to the 8 Lenses activity, where the Engagement and Critical AI Literacy lenses build directly on what they just experienced. Encourage them to bring their prompt into the 8 Lenses tool and use it as the assignment context.
