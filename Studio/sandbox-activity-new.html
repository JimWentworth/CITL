Level 1

Explore — Find the biggest themes in what we said

Run the same prompt in two tools and compare what each one finds.

Your goal: Find the three biggest themes in what your colleagues said — then see whether two different tools reach the same conclusions from the same data.

Your two tools — upload the survey file to both before you start

Both are available through the University of Illinois. Sign in with your Illinois credentials.

Microsoft Copilot (campus) Google Gemini
1

Paste this prompt into Copilot

Your prompt
I've uploaded a survey of 34 university faculty members about their concerns and hopes for using AI in their teaching. Please read it carefully and identify the three biggest themes across all the open-ended responses. For each theme: give it a short name, write one sentence describing it, and quote the single response that best captures it. Then tell me: is there anything in the data that surprised you, or that didn't fit neatly into your three themes?
2

Paste the exact same prompt into Gemini

Same prompt, same file, different tool. Don't change a word.

3

Compare the two responses

Read both side by side. Did both tools find the same three themes? Where did they agree — and where did they reach different conclusions from the same data? Which response feels more accurate to you, and why?

This is the skill: the same input producing different outputs reveals that AI tools are not neutral or interchangeable. What they emphasize — and what they miss — reflects how each model processes language and weighs evidence.
4

Send this follow-up in whichever tool gave the more interesting response

Follow-up prompt
Is there a concern in the data that only one or two people raised, but that you think deserves more attention than it's getting? What is it, and why does it matter?

🗣 Ready to share back with the group

Bring three things: the themes both tools identified, one place where they disagreed, and the under-the-radar concern the follow-up surfaced. You don't need all the answers — your observation is the contribution.

⚡ If you finish early

Go back to whichever tool gave you the more interesting themes and send it this prompt:

Extension prompt
You identified these three themes from the survey data: [paste the themes]. Now make the case that you got it wrong — what did you miss, overemphasize, or flatten? What would a more honest reading of the data say?

Notice how easily the tool argues against itself. What does that tell you about how much to trust its first response?

Level 2

Implement — Build learner personas from the data

Run the same prompt in two tools and evaluate how well each set of personas captures the real people in this room.

Your goal: Ask both tools to build learner personas from the survey data — then compare whether the personas they generate are the same, and evaluate how well either set captures the real people in this room.

Your two tools — upload the survey file to both before you start

Pick two that are new to you. Copilot and Gemini use your Illinois credentials. Claude requires a free account.

Microsoft Copilot (campus) Google Gemini ChatGPT
1

Paste this prompt into Tool 1

Your prompt
I've uploaded a survey of 34 university faculty members about their concerns and hopes for using AI in their teaching. Based on the open-ended responses, create 4 distinct faculty learner personas. For each persona include: a descriptive name (a type, not a real person), one sentence describing their current relationship to AI, their primary concern, their primary hope, and the one question they most need answered by the end of a faculty development program. Make the personas specific enough to be recognizable to someone in the room — not so broad they could describe anyone.
2

Paste the exact same prompt into Tool 2

Same prompt, same file, different tool. Don't change a word.

3

Compare the two sets of personas

Read both sets side by side. Did the tools generate similar personas or very different ones? Which set feels more true to the actual survey responses? Is there a respondent in the data who doesn't fit any persona in either set?

Why this matters: if two tools generate different personas from identical data, neither is objectively correct. The person who fits no persona is often the most important one in the room.
4

Send this follow-up in whichever tool gave the stronger personas

Follow-up prompt
Which of these personas is hardest to design a faculty development program for — and why? What would a program need to do differently to actually serve that person well?

🗣 Ready to share back with the group

Bring: one key difference between the two sets of personas, the respondent who didn't fit either set, and one implication for how this program should be designed. Be ready to say which persona you think you are — or whether you don't fit any of them.

⚡ If you finish early

Pick the persona you think best describes you and send this to whichever tool produced the stronger set:

Extension prompt
Based on the persona [paste the persona name and description], design the ideal 30-minute session for this specific person in a faculty AI development program. What would the session cover, what would they actually do, and what would they leave with? Be specific — not a generic workshop description.

This is exactly what program designers do with personas — and you'll be able to evaluate immediately whether the AI's design actually fits you.

Level 3

Build — Generate, then stress-test

Use Tool 1 to produce a synthesis document. Use Tool 2 to challenge, critique, and improve it.

Your goal: Tool 1 writes a synthesis document from the survey data. Tool 2 acts as an editor and adversary — pushing back on what Tool 1 produced, finding its weaknesses, and suggesting improvements. You direct both, then edit the final document yourself. The result should be better than what either tool could produce alone.

Your two tools

Upload the survey file to Tool 1 now. You'll open Tool 2 in Step 3 — no file upload needed for Tool 2. Choose any two from the options below.

Microsoft Copilot (campus) ChatGPT Codex Gemini Claude
1

Paste this prompt into Tool 1 (with the file uploaded)

Generation prompt — Tool 1
I've uploaded a survey of 34 university faculty members about their concerns and hopes for using AI in their teaching. Produce a one-page synthesis document with four sections: 1. Three dominant themes — each with a direct quote from the data as evidence 2. The single most important tension in this cohort's relationship to AI — stated as one sentence 3. Two concerns that appeared only once or twice but deserve more attention — and why 4. Three design principles for a faculty AI development program that responds honestly to what these people actually said — not what a program would want to hear Write it as a professional document I can share with colleagues. I will edit it before I do.
2

Read the document Tool 1 produced

Before you move on, read it carefully. Note anything that seems too vague, too obvious, or that you suspect misreads the data. You'll use Tool 2 to challenge these specifically.

Don't skip this step: the stress-test in Step 3 is only as good as your reading of Tool 1's output. The more specific you can be about what seems weak, the sharper Tool 2's critique will be.
3

Open Tool 2 and paste this stress-test prompt

Copy Tool 1's full document and paste it into the prompt below. No file upload needed for Tool 2 — the document is all the context it needs.

Stress-test prompt — Tool 2
Here is a synthesis document produced by an AI tool analyzing a faculty survey about AI in teaching: [paste Tool 1's document here] I want you to act as a critical editor. Do the following: 1. Identify the weakest of the three themes — where is the evidence thinnest or the claim most generic? 2. Challenge the central tension statement. Is it accurate, or does it flatten something more complex? 3. Evaluate the three design principles — which one is most likely to be ignored by a program enthusiastic about AI, and what would honoring it actually require? 4. Suggest one specific revision to make the document stronger. Be direct. I want critique, not reassurance.
4

Edit the document — make it better than either tool produced

Go back to Tool 1's document. Use Tool 2's critique to make at least two substantive revisions — fix the weakest theme, sharpen the tension statement, or strengthen the design principle most at risk of being ignored. The document you share with the group should be visibly different from what Tool 1 first produced.

The discipline: using one AI to critique another — then editing the result yourself — is a workflow most people haven't tried. It produces better output than any single tool and keeps you in control of the final judgment.

🗣 Ready to share back with the group

Bring your edited synthesis document. Be ready to say: what Tool 2's critique identified as the weakest part of Tool 1's draft, what you revised and why, and which design principle you think this program is most at risk of ignoring.

⚡ If you finish early

Take Tool 2's critique and feed it back to Tool 1 in the same conversation:

Extension prompt — back to Tool 1
Here is a critique of the synthesis document you produced: [paste Tool 2's critique]. Revise the document to address these specific concerns. Where you disagree with the critique, explain why and keep the original language — but justify it.

Compare this revision to the original. You've now run a full iterative loop across two tools — generating, critiquing, and revising. Notice what improved, what didn't, and where you still had to make the final call yourself.
